Cortisol is a steroid hormone made by the adrenal cortex. In everyday language people call it a “stress hormone”, but its job is broader: it helps the body handle daily energy, blood pressure and inflammation. This page is an overview for readers of CortisolTracker. It is not a diagnosis and not a dosing protocol.

## The daily pattern

In people with working adrenal glands, cortisol is usually higher in the morning and lower at night. Clinicians call the morning rise the cortisol awakening response. The exact shape of the curve varies between people and across days. A tracking app is not a laboratory assay: it does not measure the hormone in blood or saliva.

## Replacement therapy

In adrenal insufficiency the glands do not make enough cortisol. Endocrinologists prescribe **corticosteroid** replacement. Hydrocortisone is the pharmaceutical name for cortisol itself; other glucocorticoids used in replacement include dexamethasone, cortisone acetate, prednisolone, prednisone and methylprednisolone. The regimen (which drug, tablets, timing, sick-day rules) belongs to the clinician and the patient together. Changing doses because a chart “looks low” is not a substitute for that conversation.

If you have an emergency injection kit or a sick-day plan, those rules come from your care team. An informational app does not replace them.

## What CortisolTracker can show

CortisolTracker is a web app for **logging** corticosteroid intakes (the drugs above), meals and influencing factors, then drawing a **calculated** cortisol-equivalent curve for the rest of the day. That picture is meant to make timing and factors easier to discuss with your endocrinologist. It does not certify that coverage is adequate, and it does not detect an adrenal crisis.

## What it does not do

- It does not diagnose adrenal insufficiency or any other condition.
- It does not treat, prescribe, or certify replacement therapy.
- It does not replace sick-day rules, an emergency kit, or calling for medical help.
- It is not a medical device.
